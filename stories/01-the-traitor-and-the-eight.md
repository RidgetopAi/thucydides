# The Traitor and the Eight

*How the worst manager in electronics history accidentally created Silicon Valley*

---

In the fall of 1955, William Shockley chose Mountain View, California, to build his semiconductor laboratory. Not because of any strategic advantage. Not because of the nascent technology ecosystem Frederick Terman had been quietly building at Stanford for three decades. He chose it because his mother lived in Palo Alto.

It was the kind of decision that defined Shockley -- brilliant in physics, catastrophic in judgment. He had just shared the Nobel Prize for co-inventing the transistor at Bell Labs, a device that would reshape the twentieth century. But at Bell Labs, the people who actually built the first transistor -- John Bardeen and Walter Brattain -- had been so poisoned by working under Shockley that both had essentially fled. Bardeen left for the University of Illinois in 1951. Brattain requested a transfer to another group. Brattain later said he hated the famous publicity photo that showed Shockley sitting front and center at the apparatus, as if he had done the work himself.

Shockley had tried to put his name alone on the transistor patent. When his lawyer told him the prior art from Lilienfeld made that legally impossible, he told Bardeen -- the theorist without whom the device would never have worked -- "sometimes the people who do the work don't get the credit for it."

This was the man Arnold Beckman agreed to bankroll.

---

Beckman, a Caltech instrument magnate, put up a million dollars to fund Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory. He knew Shockley personally -- had been his teacher at Caltech in the 1930s. He also knew, according to later accounts, that Shockley had "no chance in the business." But he wanted Shockley's inventions.

Shockley recruited spectacularly. He lured Jean Hoerni from Caltech -- a Swiss physicist with two PhDs, from Geneva and Cambridge. He got Gordon Moore, a Caltech PhD chemist. Robert Noyce, a Grinnell College physics prodigy whose professor Grant Gale had introduced him to the transistor and once saved him from expulsion after a pig-stealing incident. Eugene Kleiner, an Austrian engineer who'd fled Vienna in 1938 ahead of the Nazis. Julius Blank, son of Jewish immigrants from Manhattan's Lower East Side. Victor Grinich, born to Croatian immigrants, the only one with actual electronics experience. Jay Last. Sheldon Roberts, a metallurgist from MIT.

They were, on average, twenty-nine years old. They had come to work for a Nobel laureate on the frontier of physics.

What they got instead was a management style that Sheldon Roberts later described with devastating precision: "When he hired you, you were the greatest person in the world, then slowly you worked your way down the line."

---

Shockley exhibited what one biographer called "reverse charisma" -- the ability to make anyone in his presence feel worse. He kept a "black book" on his employees. He abandoned the silicon transistor work that his team had been hired to do and became obsessed with a four-layer diode -- a PNPN device that one assessment called "three years ahead of its time in processing complexity." It was technically elegant and commercially suicidal. The company never turned a profit pursuing it.

The researchers tried to work around him. They couldn't. Shockley micromanaged everything while refusing to listen to anyone. By late 1956, a group of employees did something almost unheard of in that era: they wrote a letter to Arnold Beckman, the company's financial backer, asking him to remove Shockley from direct management of the laboratory.

Beckman initially agreed. He would install a professional manager between Shockley and the scientists.

Then came the Nobel Prize.

On November 1, 1956, the Swedish Academy announced that Shockley, Bardeen, and Brattain would share the Nobel Prize in Physics. Suddenly Shockley wasn't just an abrasive manager -- he was a national scientific treasure. Beckman met privately with Shockley, who convinced him to stay the course. Beckman also consulted Shockley's friends among Bell Labs' top brass, who vouched for his scientific direction.

By June 1957, when Beckman finally tried a half-measure -- inserting a manager between Shockley and the team -- it was far too late. Seven key employees had already committed to leaving.

---

In July 1957, eight men met at Gordon Moore's house. Blank, Grinich, Hoerni, Kleiner, Last, Moore, Noyce, Roberts. It was the point of no return.

They had a problem. In 1957 America, employees simply did not walk out on their employer as a group to start a competing company. There was no template for this. There was no venture capital industry. The concept barely existed.

Eugene Kleiner wrote a letter to his father's stockbroker at Hayden Stone, a New York investment bank. The letter described "a group of scientists who would be more valuable as a group." The broker, Bud Coyle, passed it to a young analyst named Arthur Rock.

Rock tried thirty-five companies. Every one of them refused.

Then Sherman Fairchild said yes. Fairchild Camera and Instrument Corporation put up $1.5 million. Hayden Stone received a 20% stake. Fairchild Semiconductor was incorporated in the fall of 1957.

Shockley called them "the traitorous eight."

The irony was exquisite: Fairchild Semiconductor's business plan was essentially the silicon transistor work that Shockley himself had abandoned in favor of his four-layer diode. They would build what he had refused to build.

---

What happened next changed the world in ways none of them could have predicted.

On December 1, 1957 -- just weeks after the founding -- Jean Hoerni opened his patent notebook and wrote down an idea for "protecting exposed p-n junctions at the surface of silicon transistors by oxide masking techniques." Robert Noyce reviewed and signed it: "Read and understood Dec 1 1957 R.N. Noyce."

It was the planar process. It would later be called "the most important innovation in the history of the semiconductor industry." By leaving a layer of protective silicon dioxide over the transistor's sensitive junctions, Hoerni solved the reliability problem that had plagued every semiconductor manufacturer. The idea drew on oxide work that Hoerni had been aware of from discussions at Shockley's lab -- knowledge that traced back through Shockley himself to Bell Labs, where Carl Frosch had accidentally discovered silicon dioxide passivation in 1955 when hydrogen ignited during a diffusion experiment.

On January 23, 1959, Robert Noyce sat down with his own notebook and extended Hoerni's insight to its logical conclusion. Pages 70-71 of his Fairchild patent notebook describe "methods of isolating multiple devices" on a single piece of silicon -- aluminum metallization lines running over the protective oxide layer to connect transistors, resistors, and capacitors into a single integrated circuit. He'd been prompted by patent attorney John Ralls, who challenged him to identify other applications for Hoerni's planar process.

Three days earlier, Noyce had met with Edward Keonjian about joint development of hybrid digital ICs for the Atlas rocket computer. The military need catalyzed the idea. But it was Hoerni's planar oxide that made it physically possible.

At Texas Instruments, Jack Kilby had demonstrated his own integrated circuit concept in September 1958 -- but using a fundamentally different approach, with hand-wired connections that couldn't be manufactured at scale. Noyce's insight was that the connections themselves could be part of the manufacturing process. This difference -- between a laboratory demonstration and a manufacturable product -- was the difference between a curiosity and a revolution.

The patent dispute between TI and Fairchild would last until 1970 and end in a cross-licensing agreement that made the question moot. By then, the integrated circuit had already transformed the world.

---

But Fairchild Semiconductor didn't survive to enjoy it.

In 1959, Sherman Fairchild exercised his buyout option. Overnight, the eight founders went from partners building something of their own to employees of a New York conglomerate. Jay Last later said the buyout "destroyed the team spirit."

The company brought in professional management. Tom Bay, a manager, clashed with Jay Last, who had been leading IC R&D at Noyce's request. In November 1960, Bay demanded the IC project be terminated, accusing Last of squandering money. Last fought alone. Gordon Moore, his co-founder, refused to help. Robert Noyce, the other co-founder, declined to discuss it.

Last left on January 31, 1961, taking Hoerni, Kleiner, and Roberts with him to found Amelco, which became part of Teledyne. It was the first crack. The diaspora had begun.

Over the next decade, Fairchild Semiconductor would hemorrhage talent and spawn an empire:

- **Signetics** (1961) -- the first company dedicated exclusively to integrated circuits
- **National Semiconductor** (1968) -- Charlie Sporck left Fairchild to lead it
- **Intel** (1968) -- Moore and Noyce, the last major founders still standing, finally left
- **AMD** (1969) -- Jerry Sanders, whom Noyce had personally recruited to Fairchild in 1961, was fired when Motorola transplants clashed with his personality. Noyce invested in AMD's founding despite running Intel.

Over twenty years, Fairchild Semiconductor spawned sixty-five companies. Economist Steven Klepper found that 82% of Silicon Valley semiconductor firms could trace their organizational DNA back to Fairchild. Eugene Kleiner went on to co-found Kleiner Perkins. Arthur Rock, the young analyst who had placed that first call for the traitorous eight, created the modern venture capital industry, inventing three fund structures still used by every VC firm today.

---

And Shockley?

Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory never turned a profit. Beckman sold it at a loss in 1960 -- deeply regretting, according to later accounts, his failure to act when the staff letter arrived in December 1956. Shockley retreated into academia at Stanford, and then descended into eugenics advocacy, spending his final decades arguing that Black people were genetically inferior. All three of his children became estranged from him. He died in 1989, alone.

The laboratory he built to house his genius -- at 391 South San Antonio Road, Mountain View, California, chosen because his mother lived nearby -- had become the seedbed of an industry. Not because of Shockley. Because of the people who fled him.

Russell Ohl, the Bell Labs researcher whose 1940 discovery of the p-n junction had made the transistor possible in the first place, retired in 1958 and moved to a small town in California. He spent his remaining years studying plant nervous systems. He said he had found the p-n junction "all by myself" and was deeply bitter about his lack of recognition. He had worked, he said, "in a rear end of a barn for so many years." He never even got a thank you.

The worst manager in the history of electronics had done more than anyone to create Silicon Valley. Not by building it -- by driving away the people who would.
